# Hiring Failure Patterns

Load when running an AZIMUTH analysis on a hiring, executive appointment, or contractor engagement decision.

**Sourcing note:** Pattern rates below are drawn primarily from Leadership IQ (2011, N=5,247 hiring managers and executives across 286 organizations) and Schmidt & Hunter (1998) meta-analysis on personnel selection validity. The Leadership IQ study methodology is not publicly disclosed; treat percentage rates as directional, not precise. Evidence base is narrower than `software-failure-patterns.md` or `ma-partnership-patterns.md`.

**Module 7 vocabulary this file supplies:** coachability as a distinct failure mode (26% of failures); interpersonal vs. technical failure rate inversion (interpersonal dimensions drive ~81% of failures; technical skill gaps drive ~11%); temperament-context fit as a structural diagnostic, not a personality judgment; skills-dominant selection bias; reference contamination mechanisms; onboarding cliff timing by role level (IC: 3–6 months; manager: 6–12 months).

---

## Pattern 1: Coachability Blind Spot

**What happens**: The candidate performs well in structured interviews but cannot incorporate feedback from managers or peers once employed. Coachability failures appear after hire, not before — no standard interview format reliably surfaces this trait. Leadership IQ: low coachability is the primary driver in approximately 26% of new hire failures studied.

**Failure signature**:
- Interview feedback emphasizes confidence and domain knowledge; no coachability-specific assessment was conducted
- Prior roles show the candidate operating largely autonomously with no documented feedback loops
- References were not asked specifically how the candidate responded to critical feedback — only whether they "take feedback well"

**Azimuth question**: What specific evidence exists that this candidate received and incorporated critical feedback from a supervisor in the past 24 months? Was any reference asked for a specific example of a direction change after feedback — not a general characterization?

## Pattern 2: Interpersonal Intelligence Substitution

**What happens**: Evaluators substitute social interview performance for operational interpersonal effectiveness. A candidate who is articulate, warm, and easy to talk to reads as high EI and collaborative — but these traits in a structured social context do not predict behavior under performance pressure, in conflict, or when delivering unwelcome decisions. Leadership IQ: approximately 23% of new hire failures trace to interpersonal intelligence gaps that were undetected in the interview process. Social interview performance is not EI under operational stress.

**Failure signature**:
- Candidate received high "culture fit" scores with no operational definition of what culture fit requires for this specific role
- No assessment scenario probed conflict, unwelcome decisions, or cross-functional friction
- References were asked about working style but not about specific interpersonal incidents under pressure

**Azimuth question**: What does interpersonal effectiveness require operationally for this role — not likability, but specific high-friction scenarios this person will face in the first 90 days? Was any interview or reference structured around those specific scenarios?

## Pattern 3: Temperament-Context Mismatch

**What happens**: The candidate's baseline operating preferences — pace, ambiguity tolerance, autonomy requirements, conflict avoidance, intensity — are structurally mismatched with the role's actual demands. This is not a personality judgment; it is a structural fit problem. A candidate suited for a scaled, process-heavy environment will underperform in an ambiguous, build-it-yourself context, and vice versa. Leadership IQ: approximately 15% of failures. Notably, technical skill gaps drive approximately 11% of failures — meaning temperament mismatches outpace technical mismatches as a failure cause, yet temperament is rarely assessed with comparable rigor.

**Failure signature**:
- Role context (startup vs. scale, build vs. maintain, high-autonomy vs. structured) was not explicitly surfaced in interviews
- Candidate's prior roles are primarily in structurally different contexts (size, stage, pace, ambiguity level)
- Interview focused on what the candidate has done, not how they prefer to operate day-to-day

**Azimuth question**: What is the operating context of this role — pace, ambiguity level, autonomy structure, political friction — and what evidence exists that this candidate has thrived specifically in that context, rather than an adjacent or structurally different one?

## Pattern 4: Skills-Dominant Selection Inversion

**What happens**: Evaluation effort concentrates on skills assessment — technical competency, domain knowledge, experience matching — which drives approximately 11% of new hire failures. The four interpersonal dimensions (coachability, emotional intelligence, temperament fit, motivation alignment) drive approximately 81% of failures but receive less structured assessment time. Selection processes are inverted relative to the actual failure distribution. Schmidt & Hunter (1998): unstructured interviews, which dominate most hiring processes, have predictive validity coefficients of ~0.38 vs. ~0.51 for structured interviews and ~0.54 for work samples.

**Failure signature**:
- Interview stages are weighted toward technical screening with a single "culture fit" or "values" conversation
- Coachability, EI, temperament, and motivation are evaluated via general impression, not structured criteria
- Assessment method is primarily unstructured conversational interviews

**Azimuth question**: Of the assessment time spent on this candidate, what fraction was structured around the four interpersonal dimensions versus technical competency? For the most critical interpersonal dimension for this specific role, what structured assessment was actually conducted?

## Pattern 5: Reference Check Contamination

**What happens**: Reference checks are the standard final validation step but are systematically biased. Candidate-selected references are pre-screened advocates. Social pressure and professional norms cause references to emphasize positives and suppress negatives — particularly for interpersonal and temperament issues, which are hard to articulate without seeming like a personal attack. Schmidt & Hunter: reference checks carry predictive validity of ~0.26, below structured interviews (0.51) and work samples (0.54). The check feels like validation but provides weak signal on the dimensions that drive the most failures.

**Failure signature**:
- All references were provided by the candidate with no independent outreach
- Reference questions were general rather than structured around specific interpersonal dimensions
- No skip-level or peer references were obtained — only references at or above the candidate's seniority

**Azimuth question**: Were any references reached independently, outside the candidate-provided list? Were references asked specifically about a situation where the candidate struggled interpersonally, received hard feedback, or underperformed — and what exactly did they say?

## Pattern 6: Onboarding Cliff

**What happens**: A new hire who would have succeeded with adequate support fails or underperforms because the organization did not provide structured integration during the productivity gap period. Individual contributors typically require 3–6 months to reach full productivity; managers 6–12 months. Both timelines are regularly underestimated, creating performance expectations the new hire cannot meet and leading to early exit or mutual dissatisfaction before the hire had a fair chance to succeed.

**Failure signature**:
- 30/60/90 day success criteria were not defined or shared with the candidate before acceptance
- The hiring manager has not committed time for structured check-ins in the first 30 days
- Onboarding plan defaults to "ramp up by meeting people" with no defined milestones or accountability

**Azimuth question**: What are the specific, measurable expectations for the first 30 and 90 days — and has the candidate explicitly agreed to them? Who owns the onboarding plan, how often will the manager meet with the new hire in the first month, and what is the escalation path if ramp is not on track?
