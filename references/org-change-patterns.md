# Organizational Change Failure Patterns

Load when running an azimuth on a restructure, reorganization, function consolidation, role elimination, or leadership transition that changes reporting lines, decision authorities, or team composition.

These patterns are structural, behavioral, and temporal failure modes that consistently evade standard change management checklists. They are intentionally distinct from the Kotter-derived failure modes documented in `references/base-rates.md` (insufficient sponsorship, middle management resistance, employee burnout, reversion after initial compliance) — those are process failures. The patterns below are structural failures that occur even when the process is followed correctly.

---

## Pattern 1: Symbolic Restructure

**What happens**: Reporting lines change; decision-making authority doesn't. The org chart updates on schedule. Behaviors, decision patterns, meeting cadences, and resource allocation remain unchanged. The restructure is declared complete and success is measured by structural conformance — not by the outcome the restructure was intended to produce.

**Why it's common**: Reporting line changes are visible, fast, and attributable to the restructure. Behavioral change is slow, diffuse, and hard to attribute to any single initiative. Leaders default to the visible metric. The gap between "the chart changed" and "decisions improved" is rarely measured.

**Failure signature**:
- Success metrics are structural (headcount in new org, reporting line conformance) rather than outcome-oriented (decision speed, cross-team collaboration, specific KPI)
- No behavioral change indicators defined at launch
- Six-month review asks "did everyone move to the new org" rather than "did the problem we were solving get better"
- The stated objective of the restructure is a behavioral or outcome goal, but no mechanism exists to track behavioral change

**Azimuth question**: What specific decision, behavior, or outcome that was failing before will be measurably different 12 months after this restructure — and how will you know?

## Pattern 2: Change Fatigue Stacking

**What happens**: This restructure lands on an organization already absorbing prior changes. Organizational capacity to absorb change is not infinite and is rarely measured before the next initiative is launched. The restructure is evaluated in isolation; the cumulative change load is not.

**Why it's common**: Each initiative is assessed on its own merits. The question "does this organization have capacity to absorb another major change right now" is structurally absent from most initiative review processes. Leaders who sponsor the change are rarely the ones absorbing it — middle management is, and their load is not measured at the sponsorship layer.

**Failure signature**:
- Two or more major organizational changes launched in the prior 18 months that are still settling
- No assessment of change absorption capacity before launch
- Middle management explicitly named as overloaded in any recent survey, feedback, or operational review
- Initiative described internally as "another one of these"
- Key talent attrition during or immediately after the prior restructure has not stabilized

**Azimuth question**: What major organizational changes have been launched in the past 18 months that are still being absorbed — and has the cumulative change load been explicitly assessed before adding this one?

## Pattern 3: Informal Authority Network Destruction

**What happens**: The formal reporting structure changes. The informal network that actually enables cross-team work — the people others call to resolve ambiguity, expedite decisions, and broker conflicts — is disrupted. The informal network is not documented, not considered in restructure design, and takes 6–12 months to rebuild after it is broken.

**Why it's common**: Restructures are designed on the formal org chart. Informal networks are invisible to org design. The people who serve as connectors, translators, and informal escalation paths often hold mid-level roles that seem fungible — and are moved, eliminated, or separated without awareness of the network function they served.

**Failure signature**:
- High performers in "connector" roles — people who bridge teams informally — moved or eliminated without analysis of their informal function
- No assessment of cross-functional relationship density before and after
- Velocity of cross-team decisions not tracked; no baseline exists to measure degradation
- The people most affected by the restructure were not consulted in the design

**Azimuth question**: Who are the 3–5 people in this organization whose informal relationships enable things to get done that the org chart does not show — and does the restructure preserve their ability to play that role?

## Pattern 4: Communication Sequencing Failure

**What happens**: Affected employees learn about the restructure from rumor, inference, or informal channels before official announcement. Trust damage is immediate and partially irreversible. Attrition in the window between rumor and announcement is disproportionately concentrated among the highest-performing employees — those with the most options.

**Why it's common**: Every restructure requires a pre-announcement circle: legal, HR, affected senior leaders, finance. Each additional person in that circle is a leak risk. The more complex the restructure, the more people must know before announcement — and the longer the window. The complexity of execution and the integrity of communication are in direct tension.

**Failure signature**:
- More than two weeks between decision and broad announcement
- More than 10 people briefed before broad announcement
- No defined Day 1 communication sequence with scripted messaging, named owners, and cascade timing
- Announcement sequencing assumes senior leaders will cascade consistently — without scripted messaging or preparation
- Communication plan finalized after the restructure design is complete, rather than concurrently

**Azimuth question**: Who knows about this restructure right now, when is the announcement scheduled, and what is the realistic probability of a leak in that window?

## Pattern 5: Behavioral Change Timeline Compression

**What happens**: The restructure is declared complete at the org chart change. Behavioral norms, meeting patterns, decision habits, and cultural expectations take 6–12 months to follow the formal structure — often longer. The gap between structural change and behavioral change is where most restructures fail silently and where reversion occurs.

**Why it's common**: Org chart changes have a clear endpoint — the chart is updated, the announcement is made, the boxes are redrawn. Behavioral change has no clear endpoint and no standard measurement mechanism. Leaders lack a mechanism to track behavioral change and default to structural completion as the success metric. The restructure is closed; the behavioral change is abandoned.

**Failure signature**:
- No behavioral change indicators defined at launch
- No 6-month behavioral review planned or resourced
- Success declared at announcement or at org chart update
- Success metrics are output-oriented (headcount, structural shape) rather than behavior-oriented (decision speed, cross-team collaboration, escalation patterns, meeting composition)
- No mechanism for affected teams to signal when the new structure is not functioning

**Azimuth question**: What behavioral indicators — not structural ones — will you measure at 6 months to determine if the restructure is working? Who owns that measurement, and is it funded?

---

## Pattern 6: Accountability Transfer Gap

**What happens**: The restructure changes who owns which decisions, budgets, and ongoing initiatives — without explicitly transferring accountability for each. Former owners no longer feel responsible; new owners have not been explicitly told they are. Decisions stall, fall through the cracks, or get made by whoever has the most confidence during the transition period.

**Why it's common**: Restructure announcements communicate the new org chart. They rarely enumerate the precise transfer of accountability for every decision domain, budget line, and in-flight initiative. The assumption is that new owners will identify their inherited responsibilities. That assumption fails when the new owner is simultaneously absorbing a new role, new reporting relationships, and a new team.

**Failure signature**:
- No formal accountability transfer process — no enumeration of what each new owner is now responsible for
- Ongoing initiatives have their owner changed on paper without a briefing to the new owner
- Budget ownership not explicitly transferred; ambiguous jurisdiction between old and new structure persists for months
- The list of decisions that change hands is not documented anywhere
- "We'll figure it out as we go" used to describe accountability for any high-stakes in-flight initiative

**Azimuth question**: For the five most consequential decisions this restructure changes ownership of — has each new owner been explicitly told they own it, and has each former owner been explicitly told they no longer do?
