# Startup Failure Patterns

Load when running a azimuth on a startup, new venture, early-stage initiative, or any plan that depends on finding product-market fit.

---

## Pattern 1: Premature Scaling

**What happens**: Team scales headcount, infrastructure, or spend before validating core demand. Burn rate exceeds revenue growth. Runway compresses before PMF is established.

**Why it's common**: Fundraising success is mistaken for validation. External pressure to "grow fast" arrives before internal evidence of repeatable demand.

**Failure signature**:
- Hiring ahead of revenue
- Infrastructure built for scale the product hasn't earned
- Marketing spend rising while conversion metrics are undefined

**Azimuth question**: Is there evidence of repeatable demand from a defined customer segment, or is growth being built on top of a hypothesis?

---

## Pattern 2: Founder-Market Mismatch

**What happens**: Founder has passion, drive, or even technical skill — but lacks deep domain knowledge in the target market. Assumptions about customer problems are built from inference, not sustained proximity to customers.

**Why it's common**: Conviction and energy are visible; domain knowledge gaps are not. Investors and advisors often can't evaluate this from the outside.

**Failure signature**:
- Customer discovery done once, not repeatedly
- Product roadmap driven by founder intuition rather than customer pull
- Sales cycle longer than expected, with unexpected objections

**Azimuth question**: How many hours has the founding team spent in direct, unscripted customer conversations? What changed in the product as a result?

---

## Pattern 3: The Solution Looking for a Problem

**What happens**: Team builds a technically impressive product around a real capability, then searches for the market problem it solves. Market validation is retrofitted, not originated.

**Why it's common**: Technical founders optimize for what's interesting to build, not what's painful for customers to lack.

**Failure signature**:
- Product definition preceded customer interviews
- "We can do X" framing dominates "customers suffer from Y" framing
- Pivot history: multiple target markets tried without traction

**Azimuth question**: What specific, named customer said this was a top-3 priority problem before any product development began?

---

## Pattern 4: Runway Miscalculation

**What happens**: Founders calculate burn rate at current headcount and current spend, then project it flat. Burn accelerates as hiring plans execute, infrastructure costs grow, and revenue takes longer to arrive. Runway compresses faster than forecast.

**Why it's common**: Optimism bias in revenue forecasts; expense forecasts anchored to current state rather than planned state.

**Failure signature**:
- Revenue model assumes aggressive conversion rates not yet demonstrated
- Expenses modeled at current headcount without full hiring plan loaded in
- No runway calculation under pessimistic scenario

**Azimuth question**: What is the runway under a pessimistic scenario (revenue at 40% of plan, burn at 120% of plan)?

---

## Pattern 5: Co-Founder Collapse

**What happens**: Co-founder relationship breaks down under pressure. Equity disputes, decision-making conflict, divergent commitment levels, or irreconcilable vision differences fracture the founding team mid-execution.

**Why it's common**: Co-founder agreements are made during optimism. Pressure-testing the relationship structure is skipped.

**Failure signature**:
- Equity split not formalized or contested
- Decision authority undefined between founders
- One founder clearly more committed (time, energy) than another
- No documented agreement on exit, vesting, or buyout mechanism

**Azimuth question**: What happens if founders disagree on a major strategic direction? Who decides? Is there a documented mechanism?

---

## Pattern 6: Market Timing Failure

**What happens**: The product is real, the team is capable — but the market isn't ready, or just became saturated, or an external shift (regulation, recession, infrastructure shift) changes the economics before traction is achieved.

**Why it's common**: Timing is hard to evaluate from inside the company. Optimism about timing is nearly universal in early-stage companies.

**Failure signature**:
- Entry point is "the market is about to explode"
- No analysis of what changed to make this the right time vs. 18 months ago
- Revenue model depends on behavior change that hasn't happened yet

**Azimuth question**: What are the 2–3 observable conditions that would confirm the market is ready? Are those conditions present now?

---

## Pattern 7: The First Customer Trap

**What happens**: First customer is signed — and that customer's requirements dominate roadmap indefinitely. The product evolves into a single-customer solution rather than a scalable platform. Subsequent customers require heavy customization to adopt it.

**Why it's common**: First revenue is celebrated; the cost of serving that revenue (customization, diversion of roadmap) is not tracked.

**Failure signature**:
- First customer represents >40% of revenue
- Roadmap priorities explained as "what our customer needs"
- Multiple prospect conversations stalled due to "missing features" that are customer-specific

**Azimuth question**: Does the product roadmap reflect what the first customer needs, or what the next 10 customers need? How do you know?

---

## Pattern 8: Distribution Absent

**What happens**: Product is built. Team believes product quality and word-of-mouth will drive growth. Distribution strategy is underdeveloped or assumed. Growth stalls at early adopters who are self-motivated; mainstream adoption doesn't follow.

**Why it's common**: Technical teams chronically underinvest in distribution. "Build it and they will come" is a default assumption, not a strategy.

**Failure signature**:
- Growth model is "organic" without defined acquisition channel
- CAC/LTV analysis not done
- No one with distribution expertise on the team

**Azimuth question**: What is the defined, repeatable customer acquisition channel? Who on the team owns it? What does the CAC look like at scale?
